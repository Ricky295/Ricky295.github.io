What bad management is actually costing you
A word to managers

Your management style might be the most expensive line in your budget

This isn't about being nice. It's about the real money that walks out the door every time you lose someone — and how much you'd save if they stayed.

How it actually works

When you treat people well, costs drop automatically. When you don't, you pay for it repeatedly — often without realising it.

→

They stop leaving

People quit bad bosses, not bad jobs. The moment they feel respected and stable, they stop looking — and your recruiting costs collapse.

→

They accept less pay

Employees at workplaces they trust consistently choose lower salaries over higher pay at places they don't. You are competing on more than money.

→

They actually work

A disengaged employee is physically present and mentally checked out. The same person, treated well, produces measurably more — without a raise.

×

The alternative

High churn → constant hiring → higher salaries to attract anyone → still lose them. The cycle repeats and you fund it entirely yourself.

Two bosses, same role, same budget

The position is identical. What differs is how they manage — and what that costs them.

❌ The bad boss

  • ↑ People leave within 12–18 months — often sooner
  • ↑ Constant recruiting, interviewing, onboarding
  • ↑ Has to pay more to attract anyone to the role
  • ↓ Low morale — staff do the minimum and wait to leave
  • ↓ Knowledge and context exits with every person
  • ↑ More sick leave, more burnout, more absence

✓ The decent boss

  • ↓ People build careers there — they don't want to leave
  • ↓ Rarely hires — when they do, it's straightforward
  • ↓ People accept below-market pay to work there
  • ↑ High engagement — people care and it shows
  • ↑ Institutional knowledge compounds year on year
  • ↓ Fewer sick days, lower burnout, longer tenure

What decent bosses actually do differently

None of this requires a budget. Most of it just requires paying attention.

01

Pay on time, without fail

Late pay tells someone their financial security is not your concern. That message lands hard and is very rarely forgotten.

02

Say what's wrong before it's too late

If someone is underperforming and you haven't told them clearly, that's a management failure — not theirs. Honest, early conversations fix most things.

03

Leave their evenings alone

Messaging people outside work hours — unless it's a genuine emergency — signals that their personal time is yours to claim. It burns people out quietly and permanently.

04

Explain your decisions

People can accept almost any call — a cut, a change, a no — if they understand why. What they can't accept is being kept in the dark like they don't matter.

05

Give credit out loud

Acknowledging someone's work in front of others costs you nothing and creates the kind of loyalty a salary bump can't replicate. People need to know they're seen.

06

Ask before you assume

When someone's output drops, ask what's going on before escalating. Nine times out of ten, it's something fixable — and a two-minute conversation is all it takes.

07

Treat people like adults

Micromanaging signals that you don't trust the people you hired. That distrust is felt immediately — and it kills initiative, ownership and morale faster than almost anything else. Give people a clear goal, get out of the way, and let them show you what they can do. If you hired right, you won't be disappointed. If you're checking in constantly, you're the bottleneck.

08

Listen to your critics

The employee who tells you something isn't working is doing you a favour. The one who says nothing and quietly resents it is the one who leaves. Criticism from inside your team is free intelligence — shutting it down just means you hear about the problems later, from someone who's already decided to quit.

The math is not complicated. The only question is whether you act on it.

Every month of high turnover is money already gone. Treating people well isn't a soft management style — it's the one that costs least and compounds over time.
